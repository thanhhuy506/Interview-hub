2. Leadership & Teamwork Questions

Focus on facts, trust, ownership, coaching, and team results—not authority.

Senior answer

When a conflict happens, I first listen to both sides and try to understand the root cause. I separate the technical problem from personal feelings and make sure everyone can explain their view respectfully.

If it is a technical disagreement, I ask the team to compare the options using clear criteria, such as requirements, performance, security, maintainability, delivery time, and operational risk. For a difficult decision, we may create a small proof of concept or record the decision in an architecture note.

After we make a decision, I explain the reason and ask the team to support it together. I also follow up later to make sure the conflict is really resolved and the working relationship is still healthy.

Short answer I listen to both sides, find the root cause, and move the discussion from personal opinions to facts. For technical conflicts, we compare options using project requirements and trade-offs, then agree on one decision as a team.

Senior answer

My goal as a leader is to help the team deliver good results and grow professionally. I start by making the goal, priority, owner, and expected result clear.

I monitor progress through regular communication, but I avoid micromanagement. I trust team members to handle their work and ask them to raise blockers early. My responsibility is to remove blockers, manage dependencies, and provide support when a problem is difficult.

I also set quality expectations for design, coding, testing, code review, and production readiness. For junior members, I give more guidance. For experienced members, I give more ownership. I try to create an environment where people can ask questions, share ideas, and learn from mistakes safely.

Short answer I manage the team through clear goals, clear ownership, open communication, and regular support. I track progress without micromanaging, remove blockers, maintain quality standards, and mentor members based on their experience.

Senior answer

I do not evaluate a developer only by the number of tickets or lines of code. I look at both technical results and professional behavior.

  • Technical quality: design, code quality, testing, and maintainability.
  • Problem-solving: ability to understand the cause and propose practical options.
  • Ownership: responsibility from requirement until production result.
  • Delivery: realistic planning, communication, and reliable completion.
  • Teamwork: code reviews, knowledge sharing, and support for other members.
  • Growth: learning from feedback and improving over time.

I also consider the person's level and assigned responsibility. A junior and a senior developer should not be measured in exactly the same way.

Short answer I evaluate technical quality, problem-solving, ownership, delivery, teamwork, and continuous improvement. I focus on business and team impact, not only ticket count.

Senior answer

I believe efficiency starts with clear priorities. The team should understand what is important, why it matters, who owns it, and what “done” means.

I encourage the team to break large work into smaller tasks, identify risks early, and communicate blockers before they become serious. Code reviews, automated tests, CI/CD checks, and clear release criteria help us maintain speed without reducing quality.

Responsibility comes from trust and ownership, not only control. I give members space to make decisions, but I also expect them to communicate progress, ask for help when needed, and take responsibility for the final result. We use retrospectives to improve the process instead of blaming individuals.

Short answer I keep the team efficient by making priorities and ownership clear, finding blockers early, using engineering automation, and improving our process regularly. I create responsibility through trust, transparency, and clear expectations.

Senior answer

In my opinion, the most important quality is ownership. A good developer does not only write code and close a ticket.

The developer should understand the requirement, think about risks, write maintainable code, test the solution, support the release, and make sure it works correctly in production. When a problem happens, the developer should help solve it instead of saying it belongs to another team.

Ownership should also include communication, teamwork, and continuous learning. Strong technical skills are important, but responsibility is what makes those skills valuable to the project.

Short answer I believe the most important quality is ownership. A good developer takes responsibility for the complete result, including requirements, code quality, testing, deployment, production impact, and communication.

3. Agile & Scrum Questions

Use correct concepts, then connect them to your real work as a senior engineer.

Senior answer

Agile is an approach to software development that focuses on small deliveries, close collaboration, regular feedback, and continuous improvement.

Instead of waiting a long time to deliver the full product, an Agile team delivers useful changes in smaller steps. The team checks the result, receives feedback, and adjusts the next plan.

Agile does not mean there is no plan or documentation. It means the plan can change when we learn new information, while the team still controls quality, risk, and delivery.

Short answer Agile is an iterative and incremental approach. Teams deliver in small steps, collaborate closely, receive feedback early, and improve both the product and the working process.

Senior answer

Scrum is a lightweight framework that helps a team solve complex product problems. Work is organized into fixed periods called Sprints. A Sprint is one month or less, and many software teams use two-week Sprints.

During each Sprint, the team works toward a Sprint Goal and creates a valuable, usable product Increment. The team regularly inspects the product and the working process, then adapts based on what it learns.

Scrum gives a clear structure, but the team still needs good engineering practices, technical skills, and communication to deliver a reliable product.

Short answer Scrum is a lightweight Agile framework. The team works in Sprints, follows a Sprint Goal, delivers a usable Increment, and regularly inspects and improves the product and process.

Correct modern terminology

Scrum defines three accountabilities inside one Scrum Team:

  • Product Owner: maximizes product value and manages the Product Backlog effectively.
  • Scrum Master: helps the team and organization understand and apply Scrum, and helps remove impediments.
  • Developers: create a usable Increment, plan the Sprint work, maintain quality, and adapt the plan each day.

The Scrum Team has no separate sub-team or hierarchy inside it. The members work together toward the same Product Goal and Sprint Goal.

Important correction The current Scrum Guide uses the term accountabilities and uses Developers, not the older separate term “Development Team.”

Senior answer

Scrum has five official events:

  • The Sprint: the main container for all other events and development work.
  • Sprint Planning: defines why the Sprint is valuable, what can be done, and how the work will be approached.
  • Daily Scrum: Developers inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt the plan.
  • Sprint Review: the Scrum Team and stakeholders inspect the outcome and discuss what to do next.
  • Sprint Retrospective: the Scrum Team plans improvements for quality and effectiveness.

Backlog refinement is also common and useful, but it is an ongoing activity rather than one of the five official Scrum events.

Short answer The five Scrum events are the Sprint, Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review, and Sprint Retrospective.

Senior answer

Most of my recent projects used Agile and Scrum, usually with two-week Sprints. I joined planning, Daily Scrum, review, retrospective, and regular backlog refinement.

As a senior developer and team leader, I did more than report my own progress. During refinement and planning, I helped clarify requirements, identify dependencies, discuss technical approaches, find risks, and estimate the work.

During the Sprint, I followed progress, supported blocked members, reviewed code, and communicated early when scope or risk changed. In reviews and retrospectives, I focused on real results and practical improvements.

I believe Scrum works best when the team is transparent and focuses on the Sprint Goal, not when members only follow meetings as a fixed procedure.

Short answer I have worked in two-week Scrum Sprints and joined all main events. As a senior and leader, I helped clarify requirements, discuss design, estimate work, manage risks, remove blockers, review code, and improve the process.

Senior answer

Agile helps the team receive feedback early and respond to changing business needs. Small deliveries reduce the risk of building the wrong solution for a long time.

It also improves collaboration between developers, QA, product members, and stakeholders. Regular reviews make progress visible, and retrospectives help the team improve its working process.

However, Agile is not automatically successful. The team still needs clear priorities, engineering quality, realistic planning, and active stakeholder participation. Without these things, Agile meetings can become only process without real value.

Short answer Agile helps teams get feedback early, adapt to change, reduce delivery risk, and improve collaboration. However, it still needs clear priorities, good engineering practices, and active communication.

Senior answer

In Scrum, the Scrum Master is one of the three required accountabilities in the Scrum Team. Therefore, someone must be accountable for helping the team understand Scrum, improve its effectiveness, and remove or address impediments.

However, this does not always mean the company needs a separate full-time job title for every team. In some organizations, one person may support more than one team, or another qualified team member may take the accountability.

The important point is that the responsibility must be clear and the person must have enough time, skill, and independence to perform it effectively.

Short answer The Scrum Master accountability is required in Scrum, but a separate full-time Scrum Master position is not always required. The responsibility must still be clearly assigned and performed effectively.
